How tightening torque is calculated

The short-form torque equation used across industry is:

T = K × F × D  where T is the torque, K the nut factor (dimensionless), F the target preload and D the nominal diameter. With F in lbf and D in inches, divide by 12 to get lbf·ft.

The target preload is a percentage of the bolt's proof load, the largest load it can carry without permanent stretch:

Proof load = As × Sp  where As is the tensile stress area and Sp the proof strength of the grade. For inch threads, As = 0.7854 × (D − 0.9743/n)², with n threads per inch.

A common practice is 75% of proof load for joints that will be taken apart and 90% for permanent joints.

Worked example: 1/2"-13 UNC, Grade 5, zinc plated

Stress area: As = 0.7854 × (0.5 − 0.9743/13)² = 0.1419 in².
Proof load: 0.1419 × 85,000 psi = 12,060 lbf.
Preload (75%): 0.75 × 12,060 ≈ 9,050 lbf.
Torque: T = 0.20 × 9,050 × 0.5 / 12 ≈ 75 lbf·ft.

K lumps together thread and under-head friction. It is the biggest source of uncertainty: the same torque on a lubricated bolt produces much more preload than on a dry one. Using a dry-thread torque on a lubricated bolt is a classic way to stretch or snap it.

FAQ

Why is the expected preload a range?

Friction varies from bolt to bolt, so torque control alone gives about ±25% scatter in preload. When preload really matters, use direct tension indicators, turn-of-nut, or measure bolt stretch.

Should I torque structural bolts (A325 / F3125) with this?

Structural steel connections follow the RCSC Specification, which prefers turn-of-nut or calibrated wrench methods and does not accept a generic torque table. Use this calculator for machinery and general bolting.

How do I convert lbf·ft to N·m?

Multiply by 1.3558. The calculator shows both, plus lbf·in for small fasteners.
